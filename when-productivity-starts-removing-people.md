# When Productivity Starts Removing People

What an AI-linked contract termination taught me about productivity, professional risk, and the changing role of technical writers in AI-assisted documentation workflows.

## The argument

- **The profession may survive while becoming smaller and less forgiving.** Productivity gains can change how companies divide work and how many people they retain.
- **Plausible output is not reliable documentation.** A generated draft still depends on context, verification, constraints, and review.
- **The role is moving into workflow design.** Technical writers increasingly shape the systems, terminology, sources, and review paths that make AI-assisted documentation usable.

## This time, the explanation was shorter. It named AI.

Companies have many ways to describe a departure without explaining the decision in much detail. The experience here was quieter and more direct.

When my contract ended in June 2025, I expected the usual language: restructuring, changing priorities, a shift in strategy. Companies have many ways to describe a departure without explaining the decision in much detail. This time, the explanation included one more factor. It named AI.

There was no press release, no public headcount, and no carefully prepared statement for LinkedIn. Nothing happened that would have attracted attention outside the company. A few people were informed, a few roles disappeared, and the organisation carried on.

That quietness made the explanation easy to dismiss. It was one person’s account, not a headline. There was no public announcement to point to and no neat story that could be repeated online. But I remember the conversation. I also remember what it felt like to hear a private explanation that the wider industry still presented as a distant possibility.

## The work remains. The jobs may not.

Two reassuring stories about AI avoid the question that matters most: what happens to the people doing the work?

AI cannot replace us because it hallucinates.

AI will not replace us because it only makes us more productive.

Both claims can be true in limited ways. Neither answers the question most professionals are actually asking: can I expect to work in this profession for the long term, or am I helping to train my replacement?

I do not know. Anyone who answers with absolute confidence is claiming more certainty than the evidence allows.

A labour market can grow while becoming less forgiving. That happens when companies decide that one person, equipped with the right tools, can take on work once shared among writers, product managers, support specialists, and developers.

In some teams, the suggested development path for a technical writer now points towards becoming a Product Builder. In practice, that means combining technical writing with product ownership, product management, development, and testing. It sounds like professional growth. It can also describe several jobs being folded into one increasingly broad role.

This is the part missing from the language of “augmentation”. The work does not disappear because a model writes the first draft. Someone still has to make the workflow usable, provide the right context, connect the necessary systems, and decide what the model should and should not receive.

If a smaller team can produce an acceptable volume of documentation with AI-assisted workflows, the company does not need to claim that AI can do every job. It only needs to decide that it needs fewer people.

The employment question does not wait for the quality problem to disappear. A tool can produce unreliable documentation and still appear productive enough to change a team’s size.

## The IT bubble is loud, but it is not universal.

The pressure to remove people from a workflow does not unfold at the same speed in every environment.

This argument also has a boundary. Much of the loudest AI discussion comes from the technology industry, where teams can sometimes move quickly from experiment to deployment and from deployment to headcount planning. That does not make the same timeline realistic everywhere.

Regulated environments often carry a different burden. A fintech team documenting a change in tax rules, or a medical-device team updating instructions, may need to preserve sources, review changes, record decisions, and keep clear responsibility for the published result. The exact controls vary, but the workflow cannot treat human review as an optional inconvenience.

The technology industry often treats the human as friction in the workflow. Regulated environments have more reasons to treat the human as part of the control system. The IT bubble should not mistake its own tolerance for speed for a universal business rule.

For technical writers, this changes the meaning of workflow design. In a lower-risk context, an agent may draft a page for review. In a regulated environment, the workflow may also need controlled sources, traceable changes, defined approval points, and evidence that someone with the right knowledge checked the result.

## Intentions are not the relevant unit of analysis.

The argument is not about whether a promise to empower workers is sincere. It is about what that promise can and cannot protect.

I have become sceptical of the technology industry’s stated intentions for a simple reason: intentions are not the relevant unit of analysis. Incentives are.

AI companies sell speed, scale, and lower labour costs. Their customers are under pressure to show the same results. The person who puts a promise that AI will “empower” workers in a press release may sincerely mean it. Still, it does not protect you when “empowerment” becomes a spreadsheet calculation.

Organisations usually know how to capture a productivity gain. They do not need another essay explaining the spreadsheet. Technical writers and leads need a clearer account of the work that makes the gain possible and the controls that keep it from becoming documentation debt.

## AI writes the document. The technical writer designs the conditions.

The visible output may be a page of documentation. The less visible work determines whether that page deserves trust.

The technical writer’s role is changing because the first draft no longer defines the whole job. AI can produce the document. The writer creates the flow that makes the document possible.

That flow may include tool servers and integrations, sometimes even something like an MCP server, along with specialised agents and the instructions that connect them. Infrastructure teams may help establish the technical foundation, but someone still has to understand what information the workflow needs and how the pieces should work together.

The writer also supplies the context that keeps the output consistent: style guidance, a glossary, preferred terminology, product constraints, examples, and the rules for handling uncertainty. This is not decoration around the writing process. It is part of the documentation system.

The result is not simply a faster author. It is a controlled path from product information to a document that someone can review, maintain, and use.

## Polished output cannot be treated as finished documentation.

The gap between a convincing answer and a correct, usable instruction remains a practical problem for technical work.

The problem is not only that AI gets things wrong. Generative models produce plausible language without knowing which detail will become expensive to fix six weeks later.

A wrong parameter does not look wrong. An omitted prerequisite does not announce itself. A procedure can read beautifully while failing at the first real edge case.

That is why the workflow matters. A style guide can reduce avoidable variation. A glossary can keep terminology stable. An integration can provide a better source of product information. A specialised agent can perform one part of the process more consistently than a general chat.

None of these elements removes the need for judgment. They make judgment easier to apply and easier to repeat.

I have also tried to build a product with AI doing much of the implementation work. The project, CitizenHub, collected official legislative changes published by Spanish authorities, interpreted their relevance, and turned them into short explanations for people who needed to understand them. That experiment exposed a different part of the problem: AI can generate momentum, but it does not automatically preserve the decisions that give a project direction.

I wrote about those lessons in [Human, but Not Humane](human-but-not-humane.html). This essay focuses on the employment question. The other one looks at what happens inside the workflow when a human asks AI to help build and maintain a product.

The connection matters here. A technical writer who designs an AI-assisted documentation workflow still has to define the sources, constraints, terminology, review points, and conditions under which the system should stop. AI can generate output. Someone still has to design the conditions for producing it and take responsibility for the result.

## The work behind the words.

The value is not only the final page of text. It is the work required to make that page accurate, consistent, reviewable, and trustworthy.

### For technical writers

- Design the workflow instead of treating AI as a faster blank page.
- Provide style guidance, glossaries, preferred terminology, and product constraints.
- Connect the workflow to reliable sources and the systems that contain product information.
- Define where agents can act, where they need human review, and where they must stop.

### For documentation leads

- Measure the quality and maintainability of the workflow, not only the number of generated pages.
- Make context design, integration work, review, and maintenance visible in planning.
- Keep ownership of product information and publishing decisions explicit.
- Do not count the human work as eliminated simply because the final draft arrived faster.

Technical writers still need product understanding, editorial judgment, and an eye for failure. But the role is no longer only about writing the page. It increasingly includes designing the path that leads to the page.

In some environments, that path may stay relatively light. In others, especially regulated ones, it may require explicit sources, approval points, traceability, and evidence of review. The workflow changes with the level of risk.

## Technical writing under AI-driven productivity pressure.

This essay looks at the economics of AI-assisted documentation work, with a focus on workflow design, verification, and responsibility rather than on generated text alone.

There is still a future for technical writers. Not because AI has failed to change the economics of the job, and not because producing a first draft has suddenly become difficult again.

The role is moving towards the design of documentation workflows. Technical writers increasingly define the context an agent receives, connect it to useful sources, coordinate specialised tools, and provide the style guidance, glossary, preferred terminology, and product constraints that shape the result.

This does not remove responsibility for the words. It moves more of that responsibility into the system that produces them.

A team may see a generated page and assume the important part happened automatically. In many cases, the important part sits one layer earlier: in the workflow, in the constraints, in the review path, and in the person who knows why the output looks the way it does.

If organisations want the output, they also need to recognise the workflow behind it. Otherwise, they may remove the person who understands why the system works and then ask the remaining system to explain itself.
