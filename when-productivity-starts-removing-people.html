When Productivity Starts Removing People

What an AI-linked contract termination taught me about productivity, professional risk, and the work technical writers can still make defensible.

Technical writing AI and work Product truth

The argument

  • The profession may survive. The number of jobs may not. Productivity gains can change how companies divide work and how many people they retain.
  • Plausible output is not reliable documentation. Technical content still depends on verification, product knowledge, and accountability.
  • The work closest to product truth remains valuable. Testing, knowledge organisation, and responsible judgment are more than just developing content.

The named reason

This time, the explanation was shorter. It named AI.

Companies have many ways to describe a departure without calling out what really happened. The experience here was quieter and more direct.

When my contract ended in June 2025, I expected the usual language: restructuring, changing priorities, a shift in strategy. Companies have many ways to describe a departure without calling out what really happened. This time, the explanation included one more flavor. It named AI.

There was no press release, no public headcount, and no carefully prepared statement for LinkedIn. Nothing happened that would have attracted attention outside the company. A few people were informed, a few roles disappeared, and the organisation carried on.

That quietness made the explanation easier to dismiss. It was one person’s account, not a headline. There was no public announcement to point to and no neat story that could be repeated online. But I remember the conversation. And I remember what it felt like to hear a private explanation that the wider industry was still presenting as a distant possibility.

Productivity is not neutral

The work remains. The jobs may not.

Two reassuring stories about AI avoid the question that matters most: what happens to the people doing the work?

AI cannot replace us because it hallucinates.

AI will not replace us because it only makes us more productive.

Both claims can be true in limited ways. Neither answers the question most professionals are actually asking: can I expect to work in this profession for the long term, or am I helping to train my replacement?

I don’t know. Anyone who answers with absolute confidence is claiming more certainty than the evidence allows.

A labour market can grow while becoming less forgiving. That happens when companies decide that one person, equipped with a chat window, can take on work once shared among writers, product managers, support specialists, and developers. Have you heard about "Product builders"?

This is the part missing from the language of “augmentation”. It is not neutral when the employer keeps the productivity gain and removes the people whose expertise made it possible.

If a team of six can produce the output once expected from ten, the company does not need to claim that AI can do every job. It only needs to decide that it needs fewer people.

Follow the incentives

Intentions are not the relevant unit of analysis.

The argument is not about whether a promise to empower workers is sincere. It is about what that promise can and cannot protect.

I have become sceptical of the technology industry’s stated intentions for a simple reason: intentions are not the relevant unit of analysis. Incentives are.

AI companies sell speed, scale, and lower labour costs. Their customers are under pressure to show the same results. The person who puts a promise that AI will “empower” workers in a press release may sincerely mean it. Still, it does not protect you when "empowerment" becomes a spreadsheet calculation.

Plausible does not mean reliable

Polished output cannot be treated as finished documentation.

The gap between a convincing answer and a correct, usable instruction remains a practical problem for technical work.

The problem is not only that AI gets things wrong. Generative models produce plausible language with the relaxed confidence of someone who will never have to maintain the system six weeks later.

A wrong parameter does not look wrong. An omitted prerequisite does not announce itself. A procedure can read beautifully while failing at the first real edge case.

That was the central lesson of building CitizenHub. The AI could be fast, articulate, helpful, and persuasive. It could also ignore a settled constraint, apologise convincingly, and get back with the same broken idea under a better name.

The issue was not simply that it made mistakes. People make mistakes too. The issue was that it carried no responsibility for the consequences and did not preserve the reasoning behind earlier decisions.

It generated output. I maintained continuity.

What technical writers can offer

The work behind the words.

The value is not only the final page of text. It is the work required to make that page accurate, useful, and trustworthy.

Product truth

  • Finding the source of truth.
  • Testing whether an instruction actually works.
  • Noticing where product behaviour and documentation diverge.

Responsible documentation

  • Organising knowledge so that others can use it.
  • Recognising uncertainty before it reaches a customer.
  • Taking responsibility when something goes wrong.

I still believe there is a future for technical writers. Not because AI has failed to change the economics of the job, and not because good writing has suddenly become difficult to generate.

The future belongs to writers who can work close to the product. They can read a specification critically, test a workflow, recognise uncertainty, organise knowledge, and explain a failure before it reaches a customer.

I know how to work with AI, but I do not mistake familiarity for trust. I can use it to accelerate research, identify gaps, and challenge a draft. I will, however, not let it replace verification, judgment, or ownership. This is the work I want to keep doing: not producing more words than a model, but making sure the words a company publishes reflect the reality. Not the AI's confidence.

Pretending that the risk is abstract would be poor preparation for what comes next.
